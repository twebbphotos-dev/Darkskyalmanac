Earth’s yearly nod. Each year Earth carries us around the Sun, so we look at Saturn from slightly different angles — a little above its ring plane, then a little below. The rings seem to nod open and shut by 2–3°, and Saturn’s shadow on the rings swaps sides around each opposition. It’s real, and you can see it month to month in the eyepiece — but it hides the big picture.

With the nod off, the slider shows the rings as the Sun sees them: one smooth 29-year cycle, from edge-on in 2025 to widest around 2032 and edge-on again in 2038–39. Turn it on to see exactly what Earth sees on each date. Tonight’s view at the top is always the real one.

More than any other planet
Saturn’s rings are countless chunks of water ice, from dust grains to house-sized boulders, spanning nearly three-quarters of the distance from Earth to the Moon yet mostly thinner than a ten-storey building. They may be young — perhaps only a few hundred million years old, though their age is still debated — and they are slowly raining down onto the planet.
