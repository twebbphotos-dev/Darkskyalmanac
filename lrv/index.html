Lunar Rover

Apollo 17 · Taurus–Littrow · December 1972

Gene Cernan and Harrison Schmitt drove this rover across the Taurus–Littrow valley on three moonwalks. The Moon's gravity is 1.62 m/s², a sixth of Earth's, and here everything follows it.

Each wheel presses down with a sixth of its Earth weight, so it grips a sixth as hard: the rover skids, bounces and leaves the ground easily. Off a crater rim it flies, and falls slowly. Dust thrown by the wheels arcs out and drops straight back, with no air to carry it.

RoverAbout 700 kg loaded with crew; 3.1 m long; four wire-mesh wheels, each with its own motor; front and rear steering Top speed18 km/h, the record Gene Cernan set on Apollo 17 (it was built for about 13 km/h on the level). Faster downhill, where gravity does the work GroundThe valley's shape and the mountains are real, from NASA's terrain model of the site, drawn at half scale across (slopes about 1.5 times real) so the whole third drive fits. The craters and rocks underfoot are generated: the model is too coarse for them

The blue dotted line is the third drive: north to the split boulder at the foot of the North Massif (stops 6 and 7), east to stop 8, and home past Van Serg crater (stop 9). The stops were surveyed from orbit; between them the line follows the general route. Your top speed counts on the line only. To beat Cernan’s 18 km/h, use the downhill run home: on the flat the motors top out just under it. Look for a fresh crater with a sharp rim, and drive off the edge. Earth hangs in the west-southwest, where it always is from here. The Seat camera puts you where Gene Cernan sat.
