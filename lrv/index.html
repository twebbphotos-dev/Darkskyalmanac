Lunar Rover

Apollo 17 · Taurus–Littrow · December 1972

Gene Cernan and Harrison Schmitt drove this rover across the Taurus–Littrow valley on three moonwalks. The Moon's gravity is 1.62 m/s², a sixth of Earth's, and here everything follows it.

Each wheel presses down with a sixth of its Earth weight, so it grips a sixth as hard: the rover skids, bounces and leaves the ground easily. Off a crater rim it flies, and falls slowly. Dust thrown by the wheels arcs out and drops straight back, with no air to carry it.

RoverAbout 700 kg loaded with crew; 3.1 m long; four wire-mesh wheels, each with its own motor; front and rear steering Top speed18 km/h, the record Gene Cernan set on Apollo 17 (it was built for about 13 km/h on the level). Faster downhill, where gravity does the work GroundThe valley's shape and the mountains are real, from NASA's terrain model of the site, but drawn at a quarter of their size across, so every distance here is a quarter of the real one: the real drives were four times as long. Heights are squashed less, so slopes come out about 1.6 times real. The craters and rocks underfoot are generated: the model is too coarse for them

Roam where you like. For a time trial, drive through the faint ring just north of the lander: the clock starts and a dotted line marks the real third drive as a loop, north to the split boulder at stop 6 (Tracy’s Rock, which Gene Cernan named for his daughter), east to stop 8 and home past Van Serg crater (stop 9). Pass each stop in turn and come back through the ring to stop the clock. Your best loop and your top speed are kept for each speed setting; to beat Cernan’s 18 km/h you will need a downhill stretch, since on the flat the motors top out just under it. Roll it hard and it breaks apart, and a new rover waits where you last drove safely. Look for a fresh crater with a sharp rim, and drive off the edge. Earth hangs in the west-southwest, where it always is from here. The Seat camera puts you where Gene Cernan sat.
